PRIMER: Information Geometry

================================================================================
FIELD OVERVIEW

Information Geometry is the study of probability distributions as points on a curved manifold, equipped with the Fisher Information metric. Rather than treating distributions as abstract objects in an infinite-dimensional space, it recognizes them as elements of a smooth manifold with intrinsic geometric structure. The core objects are: statistical manifolds (spaces of parameterized probability families), the Fisher Information matrix (defining a Riemannian metric), natural gradient flows, dual affine connections (exponential and mixture), and the notion that probability spaces have geodesics—shortest paths between distributions. Why it matters: natural gradients exploit manifold structure to converge faster than Euclidean gradients; duality reveals deep symmetries in inference (EM algorithms are geodesic flows in dual spaces); and distance measures on probability spaces (KL divergence, Wasserstein geometry) have rigorous interpretations as manifold geometry. For systems managing uncertainty, routing among models, and learning from incomplete information, this framework provides theorems that prevent waste and guarantee convergence.

================================================================================
KEY RESULTS

Natural Gradient Invariance: If theta parameterizes a distribution p(x|theta), the natural gradient is grad_N L = F^{-1} grad_E L, where F is the Fisher Information matrix and grad_E is Euclidean gradient. This quantity is invariant under reparameterization—the actual direction in probability space is independent of how you label parameters. Consequence: natural gradient descent converges faster (quadratic in favorable cases) and requires no tuning for coordinate choice.

Amari-Chentsov Theorem: The unique Riemannian metric on a statistical manifold that is invariant under sufficient statistics is the Fisher metric (up to constant scaling). This is not arbitrary; it emerges from consistency requirements.

Dually Flat Structure (Exponential Families): The manifold of exponential families admits two dual affine connections: the exponential connection (for parameterizing via natural parameters) and the mixture connection (for mixing parameters). The Kullback-Leibler divergence decomposes as a Bregman divergence with respect to the log-partition function. Mirror descent along one direction is geodesic descent along the dual.

EM as Mirror Descent: The EM algorithm is gradient descent in the dual space: E-step projects onto the dual manifold, M-step moves along a dual geodesic in the natural parameter space. Convergence is guaranteed by duality—no ad-hoc restarts needed.

Geodesic Distance and Information Bottleneck: The length of the shortest path between two distributions under the Fisher metric quantifies how hard it is to interpolate them. This connects to compression: the Information Bottleneck problem (trade-off between data compression and predictive accuracy) is geometric—you move along geodesics in the space of sufficient statistics.

================================================================================
STANDARD PRACTITIONER TOOLS

Fisher Information Matrix: compute as F_ij = E[d/dtheta_i log p * d/dtheta_j log p] or via Hessian of negative log-likelihood. Use automatic differentiation (JAX, PyTorch) to obtain it without hand-derivation.

Natural Gradient Optimizers: integrate into SGD by preconditioners; libraries like TensorFlow probability and Pyro provide Fisher-vector products. Cost is roughly 2-3x Euclidean gradient (one backward pass for score, one for Hessian-vector product).

KL Divergence and Wasserstein Distance: implemented in scipy.stats, Gromov-Wasserstein solvers in POT library. For manifold distance, approximate geodesics via Riemannian optimization libraries (Pymanopt, Geomstats).

Bregman Divergence: generalization of squared Euclidean distance for convex functions; use for custom divergences if KL is too restrictive.

Exponential Family Parameterization: work in natural parameter space (canonical form) to ensure convexity and leverage duality. Convert between mean parameters and natural parameters analytically or numerically.

HERMES APPLICATIONS

---- MODEL ROUTING OPTIMIZATION ----

(a) Mathematical Structure:
The set of candidate models (Claude, GPT, Grok, Mistral, Mistral-Small) can be represented as a mixture distribution over output spaces. Each model induces a conditional distribution p(output | input, model). The Hermes routing policy is a distribution over model choices given the task. Model routing is optimization on a statistical manifold: you want to select the distribution over models that maximizes expected utility (quality, speed, cost) subject to constraints. The space of mixture weights forms a simplex—a curved manifold with boundary. Natural gradients on the simplex are well-defined and converge to optimal mixtures.

(b) Current Approach:
Hermes uses heuristic rules: task type maps to a hand-coded model preference list, sometimes with fallback based on availability or latency threshold. No principled learning from past routing decisions.

(c) Principled Approach:
Treat model quality on past turns as samples from a Dirichlet-Multinomial model. Maintain a posterior distribution over model quality (success rate, latency, cost). On each new subtask, compute the natural gradient of expected task reward with respect to the mixture weights. Update the routing policy as: w_new = w_old + eta * F^{-1} * grad_E[reward]. The Fisher matrix here is the Fisher metric on the simplex of model mixtures. This automatically trades off exploration (high entropy) and exploitation (concentrating on best models) via the natural metric. Implement via a small neural network that predicts (quality, latency, cost) for each model conditioned on task features, then solve the mixture problem once per task.

(d) Value Assessment:
WORTH IMPLEMENTING for high-frequency routing. Why: the math directly applies (finite discrete space, clear reward signal, rapid iteration). Effort: ~2 weeks (posterior inference, natural gradient on simplex, integration with routing loop). Risk: transfer risk is LOW because the manifold structure is rigorous for discrete mixtures. Caveat: you must reliably measure routing outcomes; noisy rewards break the signal. If task quality is subjective or delayed (e.g., user satisfaction after 1 hour), the benefit shrinks.

---- PROBABILITY DISTRIBUTION GEOMETRY IN UNCERTAINTY ESTIMATES ----

(a) Mathematical Structure:
When Hermes aggregates predictions from multiple models (hermes-swarm-consensus), it constructs a mixture or ensemble of output distributions. Each model outputs p_i(output | input); aggregation produces a combined distribution. The space of all such distributions is a statistical manifold. Consensus quality depends on how "spread out" the model predictions are in this manifold—high KL divergence between models signals disagreement. Geodesic distance (Fisher metric) quantifies the true difficulty of averaging predictions.

(b) Current Approach:
Hermes averages logits or probabilities, or uses majority vote. Uncertainty is estimated via entropy of the averaged distribution. No explicit use of manifold geometry; consensus is essentially Euclidean averaging in logit space.

(c) Principled Approach:
Compute the geodesic mean (Fréchet mean) of the model distributions under the Fisher metric. This is the point on the manifold closest (on average) to all models. For categorical outputs (discrete tasks), this is well-defined and can be solved via Riemannian optimization. Use the variance of models around this mean (intrinsic variance) to estimate confidence: high intrinsic variance = high aleatoric uncertainty. For continuous outputs, if models output Gaussians, the geodesic mean corresponds to optimally weighted mixture (not simple averaging). Compute via: mu_consensus = argmin_{mu} sum_i w_i * KL(p_i || p_mu), where p_mu is a reference distribution. Weights w_i can be learned (model reliability) or uniform. The geodesic distance from each model to consensus reveals which model is an outlier.

(d) Value Assessment:
CONDITIONALLY WORTH IMPLEMENTING. Why: the math correctly models the geometry of disagreement. Effort: ~3 weeks (implement Riemannian solver for Fréchet mean, integrate into consensus loop, validate that intrinsic variance predicts downstream errors). Risk: MEDIUM transfer risk. The Fréchet mean is well-defined in theory but computationally expensive (iterative optimization per consensus call); for high-frequency consensus (100+ calls per turn), this overhead may exceed latency budget. Benefit is clearest when model disagreement is informative—if all models usually agree or disagree randomly, geometric averaging adds little. Mitigation: pre-compute Fréchet mean on a subsample; use fast approximations (e.g., log-linear averaging for exponential families).
